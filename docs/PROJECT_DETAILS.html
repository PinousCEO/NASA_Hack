Mission Forge is a space-mission design game for one to four players, on the web and in mixed reality. Play it now, with a nickname only: https://mission-forge.onrender.com

In one sentence: you say what you want, an AI builds it onto your spacecraft while your crew watches, and the physics tells you what it cost.

What you do in a mission

  • Pick a mission. A board of missions on Earth, the Moon and Mars, each with its own place drawn from NASA imagery and elevation data. An AI writes a new one every day. Three levels: Cadet, Engineer, Chief.
  • Set your goal. Your crew says what it is going for; that decides which science counts most.
  • Build the spacecraft. Tell your AI in your own words ("Add a camera that can see algae in the water") or take parts from the shelf. A part works only once its power, data and control lines are connected.
  • Balance the resources. Ten live tiles show mass, power, budget, data, link and schedule. Change one thing and everything that depends on it is recalculated, with one line saying why the number moved.
  • Choose an orbit and a launch vehicle. A design the rocket cannot lift to that orbit cannot fly.
  • Fly it. A year is simulated day by day, with events such as a solar storm or a failed battery cell. Four conditions say whether the mission works, and why.
  • Try again, compare, compete. Change the design and fly again, save and compare designs, and see where your crew ranks among crews on the same mission.

What is new

  • Say it, and it is built. The AI is not an adviser beside the game; it is how you build. If the part you ask for is not in the catalogue, it designs one and assembles a 3D model for it.
  • No single right answer. Hints and AI answers lay out the options and what each costs. They never pick for you. Low power, low cost or bold: different designs finish the same mission.
  • Mixed reality for access. On a Meta Quest 3, with bare hands, the mission floats above your open palm. Turn the planet, point at a place, speak. A child who cannot read a link budget can still hold the spacecraft and say an idea out loud.
  • A crew that sees each other. Where anyone points, with a finger in the headset or a mouse on the web, everyone sees a mark at the same place. Each player holds a role with its own priority, and each objective is credited to whoever solved it.
  • Nobody plays alone. Empty seats can be filled by AI crewmates, so a solo student still meets the negotiation between roles.
  • A game that grows. Every crew's solution is saved, and the next crew's AI can draw on it.

Why it matters

Students rarely get to try a whole mission from idea to operation. Here one decision visibly moves everything else, in language a beginner can use, and a failed mission is explained instead of just scored.

How it was built

  • Server: TypeScript on Node.js, holding the one authoritative state over HTTP and WebSocket.
  • Client: Next.js, React and Three.js; WebXR through the Immersive Web SDK. No app install on the headset.
  • Engineering models: orbit, eclipse, power, link budget, data, mass and launch capacity. They are deterministic and tested against reference values. Cost, schedule and launch-vehicle figures are game models and are labelled as such.
  • AI, kept in check: whatever the language model writes is treated as data. It is checked, built in a test copy, tested, and only then put into the shared mission.
  • Tested: 159 automated tests and 51 browser checks; mixed reality tried on a real Quest 3.

Limits

All missions are orbiters with an optional rover. The flight is simulated and shown as a result, not played through. The interface is in English.
